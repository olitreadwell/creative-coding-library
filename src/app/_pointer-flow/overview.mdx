## What this is

Pointer Flow fills the canvas with a small group of large, soft balloons. Each
balloon drifts gently on its own and reacts to your cursor: in attract mode they
float toward your hand; in repel mode they shy away. When you click, a bright
ring blasts outward from the click point. As the ring sweeps over each balloon,
it shoves the balloon hard and squashes it flat for a moment before it springs
back to its round shape.

The result looks punchy because the feedback is immediate and physical: the ring
expands fast, every balloon in its path reacts at the right moment, and the
spring rebound makes each hit feel satisfying.

## The big idea in plain words

The sketch runs two loops on every animation frame:

1. **Physics step.** For each balloon, add up the forces acting on it (cursor
   pull or push, shockwave impulse, soft nudge away from neighboring balloons),
   apply a small amount of friction, cap the speed, then move the balloon by its
   velocity times the elapsed time.

2. **Draw step.** For each balloon, compute a ring of points around its center
   with a tiny sine ripple on the radius, connect them with smooth curves, fill
   with a palette color, and paint a small radial gradient highlight to give the
   illusion of a rounded surface.

The shockwave is a simple expanding ring. Its impulse on a balloon is strongest
when the ring edge lines up exactly with the balloon center, and falls off
quickly on either side. That timing is what makes each balloon appear to get
"hit" rather than all moving at once.

## Why it matters

The attract/repel pattern and the shockwave impulse model are the same building
blocks used in games for area-of-effect explosions, fluid simulations, and crowd
steering. The squash-and-stretch spring is the same trick animators use to show
weight and elasticity. Learning these three pieces together gives you a practical
toolkit for interactive motion.
