## What this is

In the 1780s, Ernst Chladni drew a bow across the edge of a metal plate sprinkled with sand. The plate hummed, and the sand jumped away from the buzzing parts and settled into still lines, forming startlingly precise patterns. This sketch recreates those patterns with math: bright curves mark the places on a vibrating square plate that never move.

## The big idea in plain words

When a plate vibrates, not every spot shakes. Some lines stay perfectly still. They are called **nodes**, and on a real plate the sand piles up there because it gets shaken off everywhere else.

A vibrating plate does not pick one pattern. It rings with several **modes** at once, each a simple grid of ripples, and they add together. This sketch sums two of them:

- one wave with `a` ripples one way and `b` ripples the other,
- plus a second wave with those numbers swapped.

Wherever the two waves cancel out to zero, you get a node, and we draw a glowing line there. Change `a` or `b` by one and the whole pattern reorganizes into a new figure: a grid, a star, a flower, a lattice.

The lines drift and shimmer slowly because the sketch lets the two mode numbers wander a hair around their targets, the way a real plate is never perfectly tuned.

## Why it matters

Chladni figures are one of the most beautiful demonstrations in physics, and they teach an idea that reaches far past sand:

- **Superposition.** Complicated behavior is often just simple waves added together. The same rule explains musical chords, ocean swells, radio signals, and the colors in a soap bubble.
- **Standing waves and resonance.** Every drum, guitar string, wine glass, and even a building has natural modes where it prefers to vibrate. The nodes here are the same nodes an engineer studies to keep a bridge from shaking apart.
- **Pattern from constraint.** Nobody designs these figures. They are forced by the shape of the plate and the math of waves. That is the same quiet logic behind much of the structure in nature.
