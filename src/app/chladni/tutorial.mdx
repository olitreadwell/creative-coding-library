# Tutorial: Chladni Figures

By the end of this tutorial you will understand how one formula produces every pattern you can see, and why equal mode numbers create a diagonal figure.

## Predict

The formula for the Chladni figure adds two products of sine functions. Each sine product creates a grid of ripples. When the two mode numbers `a` and `b` are equal, the two terms become identical.

If both mode numbers are equal, what shape do you expect the nodal lines to form? Think about whether the figure will have diagonal or horizontal symmetry.

## Run

1. Open the sketch and set `a = 2, b = 3`. Study the pattern for five seconds.
2. Change `b` to 2 (equal to `a`). Watch the figure reorganize. Compare the shape.
3. Now change both to `a = 4, b = 4`. The diagonal structure becomes more complex.
4. Change `a` to 5 while keeping `b = 4`. The symmetry breaks. A new figure appears.

## Look closer

- The plate's rim is always a bright line. The formula guarantees it: every sine term is zero at the edges, so the edge is always a node.
- Swapping `a` and `b` produces the same figure rotated 90 degrees. That follows from the formula's structure.
- The lines breathe slowly because the sketch lets the mode numbers drift a tiny amount around their slider values. This mimics how a real plate is never perfectly tuned.
- The figure is computed on a 200 by 200 grid, not pixel-by-pixel. The small grid is then scaled up. That is why the rendering is fast.

## Break it

- Try `a = 1, b = 1`. This is the simplest possible figure: two diagonal lines crossing the plate.
- Try `a = 6, b = 7`. Count the segments inside the plate.
- Try `a = 3, b = 5`, then swap to `a = 5, b = 3`. Compare the two figures.
- Look at the corners of the plate. Are they always nodes? The formula predicts they should be.

## Recap

One trigonometry formula, evaluated at every point on a grid, produces every figure in this sketch. The pattern you see is exactly where that formula equals zero.
