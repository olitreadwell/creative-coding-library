## What this is

Conway's Game of Life is a simulation that runs on a grid of cells. Each cell is either **alive** or **dead**. Every tick, the grid updates based on four simple rules. Those rules decide which cells survive, which die, and which are born.

The result looks alive. Patterns glide across the screen. Clusters pulse and shift. Some shapes repeat forever. All of it comes from four rules and a starting arrangement of cells.

## The big idea in plain words

Think of the grid like a neighbourhood. Each cell has eight neighbours: up, down, left, right, and the four diagonals.

Each tick, the grid checks every cell and applies four rules:

- A live cell with **fewer than 2** live neighbours dies. (Too lonely.)
- A live cell with **2 or 3** live neighbours survives. (Just right.)
- A live cell with **more than 3** live neighbours dies. (Too crowded.)
- A dead cell with **exactly 3** live neighbours comes alive. (Born.)

That is everything. Count the neighbours, apply the rule, move to the next generation.

## Why it matters

Conway's Game of Life introduced several ideas that matter in programming and science:

- **Cellular automata.** A system where simple local rules produce complex global behaviour. Many natural processes (crystal growth, traffic flow, biological patterns) follow this shape.
- **Emergence.** The rules say nothing about "gliders" or "oscillators". Those patterns emerge on their own. Nobody designed them. They appear because the rules allow them.
- **Turing completeness.** The Game of Life can simulate a full computer. You can build logic gates, memory, and loops out of nothing but cells.

You do not need to understand any of that to enjoy watching it run. Hit Play and see what the rules create. The Tutorial tab shows how the code works, step by step.
