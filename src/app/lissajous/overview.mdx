## What this is

A Lissajous curve is a shape traced by a point that moves in two directions at once. Both directions follow a sine wave. The resulting path loops and crosses itself in ways that look almost musical. This sketch draws that shape as a glowing line that slowly morphs over time.

## The big idea in plain words

Think of two pendulums. One swings left and right. The other swings forward and back. Attach a pen to the point where they meet, and the pen draws a Lissajous curve.

In code, we replace each pendulum with a sine wave:

- **x position**: `A * sin(a * t + phase)`
- **y position**: `B * sin(b * t)`

Here `a` and `b` are the **frequencies** of the two waves. Their ratio is the key. A ratio of 3:2 means one wave completes three cycles while the other completes two. That ratio sets the basic shape.

The **phase** is a time offset between the two waves. When the phase is zero the curve is a stable, symmetric figure. As the phase changes, the shape rotates and stretches. The sketch advances the phase a tiny amount each frame, so the curve never stops moving.

A **decay** factor makes the curve fade toward the center over many cycles, which gives it the look of a harmonograph drawing.

## Why it matters

Lissajous curves show up in:

- **Music and signal processing.** Oscilloscopes display them when you feed two audio signals into the X and Y inputs. Musicians and engineers use them to check whether two signals are in tune or in phase.
- **Math education.** They are one of the clearest examples of a **parametric curve**: a shape where both x and y are defined by a shared variable `t`, not by a single equation `y = f(x)`.
- **Art and design.** The shapes are naturally pleasing. They appear in logos, visualizations, and generative art.
