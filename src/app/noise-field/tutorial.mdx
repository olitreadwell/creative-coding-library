# Tutorial: Noise Field

By the end of this tutorial you will understand how Perlin noise becomes a direction, how particles follow that direction, and why overlapping trails glow.

## Predict

Perlin noise at position (x, y) returns a smooth number. The sketch multiplies that number by `TAU * 2` (about 12.6) to convert it to an angle in radians. Radians are a way to measure direction: 0 means "right," 3.14 means "left."

If the noise value at a point is 0.5, what angle does that produce? (Multiply 0.5 by 12.6.) Does that direction point roughly right, up, left, or down?

## Run

1. Open the sketch and watch the particles for fifteen seconds. Identify two or three places where trails concentrate.
2. Click "New seed" and find where the trails concentrate in the new field. Notice that the concentration spots are in different places.
3. Watch a single particle for several frames. It follows a curved path, not a straight line, because the direction field changes from cell to cell.
4. Let the sketch run for a full minute without interruption. Watch the glowing regions grow.

## Look closer

- The canvas is never fully cleared each frame. Instead a nearly-transparent dark rectangle is painted over it, dimming old trails slightly. Over many frames old trails fade, while heavily visited paths stay bright.
- The blend mode is set to `"lighter"` before drawing particles. This makes overlapping colors add rather than cover. Ten dim strokes at the same spot produce one bright spot.
- 2000 particles are created at random positions. Each particle is stored as an `{ x, y }` pair with no velocity. The flow field provides all the direction.
- Particles that move off the edge of the canvas wrap to the opposite edge (toroidal boundary). No particle is ever lost.

## Break it

- Try changing the field scale in the source. A smaller scale value means the noise changes slowly, so particles take long sweeping paths. A larger scale means tight, turbulent spirals.
- Try running with 500 particles instead of 2000. The trails become sparse and thread-like.
- Watch what happens immediately after clicking "New seed." The old trails fade rapidly but are still visible for a moment.
- Notice that particles always move in the direction the field points, never backward. The field only determines direction, not speed.

## Recap

Perlin noise gives each location a smooth angle. Particles follow that angle step by step. Additive blending makes paths glow where they overlap. Those three ideas, combined, turn 2000 moving dots into a luminous flowing field.
