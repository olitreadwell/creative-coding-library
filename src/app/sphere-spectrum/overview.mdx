## What this is

A single sphere, turning slowly. At rest it is smooth and cold blue: it does
nothing until it hears something. Turn on the microphone and its surface erupts,
pushing out into colored peaks that track what you make.

Two things follow loudness:

- **Height**: quiet sounds leave the surface smooth; loud sounds push that part
  of the sphere out into a spike.
- **Color**: quiet is cold (blue), loud is hot (red), running through a rainbow in
  between.

Frequency wraps the sphere as rings of latitude: bass near the top, treble near
the bottom, so different pitches light up different bands of the surface.

Nothing leaves your browser. The mic only starts after you press the button.

## The big idea in plain words

A microphone signal can be split by the Web Audio FFT into bins, each bin the
loudness of one pitch. Raw bins are misleading: because pitch is logarithmic, the
bass crams into the first few bins while the treble sprawls across hundreds. So we
group the bins into a couple of dozen perceptual bands on a log scale, with a
gentle boost to the highs so cymbals are not drowned by the kick.

Every point on the sphere belongs to one band, chosen by its latitude. Each frame
that band's loudness pushes the point outward and sets its color. A fast-attack,
slow-release envelope per band keeps the motion punchy without flickering, and a
noise gate ignores room tone so faint sounds leave the sphere still.

## Why it matters

Audio-reactive visuals are a staple of music videos, live visuals, and
installations. The reusable pattern here is the audio pipeline: log-frequency
banding, loudness shaping, and a peak-decay envelope turn a raw FFT into a stable,
musical signal. Drive any geometry from those bands (a sphere here, but equally
bars, petals, or particles) and you have a new instrument.
