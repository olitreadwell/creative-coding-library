# Tutorial: Sphere Spectrum

By the end of this tutorial you will understand how each vertex on the sphere is assigned to a frequency band and how the envelope makes the response feel punchy.

## Predict

Each vertex is assigned a band by its latitude (its y direction from the center). Band 0 is at the top (bass). The highest band is at the bottom (treble).

If you sing a high note, which part of the sphere do you expect to spike: the top, the bottom, or the equator? (A high note is treble, not bass.)

## Run

1. Click "Enable" and allow microphone access.
2. Hum a low note for two seconds, then a high note for two seconds. Watch which latitude spikes for each.
3. Snap your fingers once near the microphone. Notice how quickly the spike rises and how slowly it decays.
4. Set the audio slider to maximum and hum. The spikes should be more dramatic.

## Look closer

- The sphere geometry is computed once at startup. For every vertex the sketch pre-computes two things: the unit direction vector (which way the vertex points from the center) and the band index (which latitude ring it belongs to). These are cached so the per-frame loop stays fast.
- Log-frequency band edges are computed with `edges[k] = round(1 * (hi/1)^(k/N))`. This geometric spacing gives each band roughly the same perceptual pitch range, rather than giving bass almost no bins and treble hundreds.
- The envelope formula is `env = prev + (target - prev) * rate`. When the target is above `prev`, the rate is fast (attack). When below, the rate is slow (release). This is what makes spikes rise fast and decay gradually.
- After moving vertices, `geometry.computeVertexNormals()` is called. This recalculates how the surface faces the light, so the lighting follows the new displaced shape.

## Break it

- Enable the mic in a quiet room with "audio" at maximum. Even room tone may cause small, constant spikes.
- Enable noise gate and compare. The sphere should stay smooth when no intentional sound is present.
- Whistle a constant pitch and watch the sphere hold a spike at one latitude.
- Clap with both hands far from the mic and then close. Compare the spike intensity.

## Recap

Each vertex is assigned a frequency band by latitude. Every frame, that band's loudness pushes the vertex outward and shifts its color. An attack/release envelope makes the response punchy. Refs hold the audio data so the React tree does not re-render 60 times per second.
